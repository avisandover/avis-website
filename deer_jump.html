Deer Jump Reservation

History

Long before European settlers came to Andover, the Penacook Indians traveled up and down the Merrimack River, fishing and hunting game. A Penacook village was located on Pine Island, and burial grounds have been found near the riverbank. Local Indigenous artifacts can be seen at the Phillips Academy Peabody Museum.

During the 17th and early 18th centuries, ferries carried passengers from Deer Jump across the Merrimack. Deer Jump Falls once powered a grain and saw mill. During the mid-1800s, after the success of the textile mills in Lowell, the Essex Company considered building a large dam at Deer Jump to provide power for a rival mill. Instead, they built their 900-foot-long dam at Lawrence, flooding out Deer Jump Falls, but leaving much of the riverfront undeveloped. In the early 1900’s, barges towed tourists along the scenic Merrimack River to enjoy the cool breezes and views.

Through the efforts of Harold Rafton, Andover’s renowned conservationist, Deer Jump was purchased between 1960 and 1973. Mr. Rafton considered the acquisition of Deer Jump Reservation “the most ambi - tious and most rewarding” of all AVIS undertakings.

In the spring of 2017, AVIS purchased 26 acres of the former Franciscan Seminary land nearest to the river to permanently protect the trail and steep riverbank. This land is now known as the Susan Stott Reservation. Walter Kittredge, senior curatorial assistant at the Harvard University Herbaria, wrote: “I first walked this section (the Franciscan property) on Labor Day in 1982 as a young botanist, and was immediately impressed with how different this forest felt, as though one had entered a cathedral, with its towering arched trees. The openness of the forest added to the effect as the long, large trunks stood out, their crowns far overhead. This mature stand of Oak-Pine-Hemlock Forest is uncommon in our area, requiring cool, steep north-facing seep slopes like this one

“There are mature black and paper birches scattered throughout the forest, indicating that either a fire occurred, or a large blowdown, perhaps from the 1938 12 hurricane. At this point in time, there are very few saplings, which is typical in mature forests.

“During my initial visit, I found two beautiful ferns that were unfamiliar to me, Silvery Glade Fern and Long Beech Fern, as well as Maidenhair Fern, all of which are locally rare. I also saw Bluebells on a sunny riverbank, fruiting plants of both Red and White Baneberry, and the variegated leaves of Downy Rattlesnake Orchid. And of course, one of my favorite trees, the Striped Maple, with its broad leaves and white striped bark. All of these plants are indicators of a very rich and unusual flora. In my forty years of field work on local floras, I don’t know of another place that matches it.”

In 2016, George Behrakis generously donated a six-acre triangle of land bordering the Tewksbury town line, thus completing the Deer Jump Trail from beyond the Fish Brook pumping station upstream to the western town line.

Natural features

More than 10,000 years ago, glaciers shaped Deer Jump Reservation’s steep river banks and left behind striated boulders. Torrents of water pouring from the melting glacial ice deposited sand and clay in outwash plains. Stagnant ice left behind step-like deposits of glacial sediments known as kame terraces.

This long, narrow reservation meanders through numerous pockets of diverse forests. Some show “old growth” characteristics, with mature and young trees intermixed with healthy understory plants, rotting fallen trees, and pockets of sun and shade. Other sections favor stands of birch and beech, maple and hickory. Majestic oaks, basswood, hickory, sycamore and yellow birch line the riverbank. And stands of towering hemlock and white pine create deep pockets of shade. You may glimpse bald eagles soaring overhead, and spot White-tail deer, red fox, turkeys, Great Blue Herons, and numerous songbirds. Spring wildflowers like bloodroot, trillium and Solomon’s seal are followed by goldenrod, asters and other pollinator plants, particularly in the meadows by Ravens Bluff, Jillian Way, and Brundrett Road.

Bay Circuit route and Merrimack River Trail

Several trails coincide in this area. The Pennacook Trail from Lawrence to Lowell Heritage State Park (envisioned in a 1988 study by the National Park Service) is part of the larger Merrimack River Trail. The Bay Circuit Trail enters Deer Jump Reservation from Launching Road. Bear left at the first trail junction, toward the river. Turn left (southwest) and continue along the Merrimack River for approximately 2.5 miles, sometimes hugging the riverbank and sometimes traveling high above it. Blazed trails to the left lead to street access and parking on several residential streets off River Rd. The trail goes over several plank bridges and then crosses National Grid Company property through a stand of mountain laurel and birches. The trail narrows after the power line and continues along steep terrain. A bench allows a rest stop beside the river, and side trails connect upland areas and a parking lot adjacent to Francis Drive. No parking at the Melmark School, please. The trail next passes through the Susan Stott, Spalding, and Behrakis Reservations (both AVIS properties) and on into Tewksbury.

North and east of Launching Rd., the wide smooth Merrimack River Trail extends to the end of the Philips Medical Systems complex, through the Albert Retelle Reservation, offering good views of the river. This section of the trail is popular for hiking and jogging. The Merrimack River Trail then narrows and continues under Rte. 93 and to the Lawrence line. The trail will eventually lead to Heritage State Park in Lawrence and beyond.

Suggested walks

Any segment of this long trail done point-to-point will yield enjoyable walking and beautiful river views. Several shorter loop trails are also popular.

Blackbirch Loop: East from the Tewksbury Water Treatment Plant, bear right uphill on the Hemlock Trail, follow Blackbirch Trail back down, and return via the main Merrimack River trail. 2.2 miles, 174 feet of elevation.

Nollet Loop: From Nollet Circle, follow signs and a few white blazes. 0.5 mile, 62 feet elevation change.

Rafton Memorial Loop: East from Launching Road: wide and mostly unobstructed 1.0 mile trail through groves of white pine. Elevation is a gentle 50 feet
